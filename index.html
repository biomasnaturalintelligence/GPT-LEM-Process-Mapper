GPT/LEM, Explained

An interactive guide to seeing the world as processes — not things

1 of 8

Step 1The Big Idea

Most of us describe the world using nouns: a forest, a village, an economy. GPT/LEM asks you to flip that — and describe the world as processes instead.

A "forest" sounds like a stable thing. But really, a forest is a slow, ongoing dance of photosynthesis, decomposition, water cycling, and tree-growth happening all at once. Stop the processes, and the "forest" disappears.

This framework gives you two tools:

GPT — General Process Theory. A vocabulary for describing what kind of process something is. Five types (T1–T5).
LEM — Leveled Mereology. A way to organize processes into nested levels — small processes are parts of bigger processes, and new properties emerge at each level.

Together they let you map a complex situation — a territory, an ecosystem, a community — and see what's actually happening, at what scales, and where the leverage points are.

Developed by philosopher Johanna Seibt. Used in ecology, sustainability, organizational design, and territorial planning.

Step 2Why "Processes" Instead of "Things"?

Try this thought experiment.

Your body. Is it a thing? It feels like one. But every cell in your body will be replaced within ~7–10 years. The atoms in your blood today were in your dinner last week. So what is your body, exactly?

It's a pattern of processes — metabolism, repair, breathing, circulation — that hold a recognizable shape over time. Stop the processes for a few minutes, and the "thing" is gone.

The same logic applies to anything alive or complex: a coral reef, a city, a marriage, a company, a forest. They look like things. They behave like processes.

Why this matters practically

If you treat a forest as a thing, you might count the trees and call it managed. If you treat it as a set of processes, you ask: Is photosynthesis still happening at scale? Is the seed-dispersal process intact? Is the soil-microbiome process recovering or collapsing?

The questions you can ask depend on the vocabulary you have. GPT gives you a richer vocabulary.

Step 3The Five Types of Processes

GPT classifies processes into five types. Click each one to see what it means.

Click any type above to see how it works ↑
The crucial distinction: A T1 process (Activity) is "already happening" at any moment you observe it. A T4 process (Development) "goes somewhere" — it has phases and you cannot understand it from a single snapshot. Mixing these up is the most common analytical error.

Step 4Practice: Classify These

Try to identify the type. There's often more than one defensible answer — pick the most dominant.

🌬️ Wind blowing across a field.
🌳 A forest recovering from a fire over 40 years.
💧 The water in a watershed (considered as substance).
🏘️ A village that has existed for 200 years.
🪣 The 50 liters of rainwater collected in a tank yesterday.

Step 5The Five Parameters of a Process

Once you've classified a process, GPT asks you to describe it along five dimensions. Together these give you a "profile" of how the process actually works.

PARAM 1
Automerity

Is a small slice of the process the same kind of thing as the whole?

A river flowing for 1 second is still "river flowing." But the first 5 minutes of baking bread is not "baking bread" — it's just mixing.
PARAM 2
Participants

Who or what is involved, and in what role? (Agent, patient, instrument, medium, setting.)

In "fishing," participants are: fisher (agent), fish (patient), net (instrument), water (medium), reef (setting).
PARAM 3
Composition

What smaller processes make this process up?

"Photosynthesis" is composed of light absorption + water splitting + carbon fixation + sugar formation.
PARAM 4
Shape

How does it unfold? Cyclic, linear, gradual, sudden, cascading, rhythmic?

A tide is cyclic. A landslide is sudden. A child growing is gradual.
PARAM 5
Context

What larger process surrounds and enables this one?

"Fishing" is embedded in the larger process of "village food economy," which is embedded in "regional ocean ecology."
Why these matter: If you only know the type of a process, you have a label. If you know all five parameters, you understand how it actually behaves and where to intervene.

Step 6LEM — Stacking Processes Into Levels

Processes nest inside other processes. LEM (Leveled Mereology) gives you a clean way to organize this. Click each level to expand.

N1Foundational processes▾
The smallest building blocks. Individual metabolisms, biogeochemical cycles, basic transactions.
Examples in a coastal village: a single fish breathing, a coral polyp feeding, one person buying bread.
N2Meso-level integrations▾
Many N1 processes combine into community-scale dynamics.
Examples: the reef ecosystem function, the village daily market, the school of fish migrating.
N3Systemic / territorial level▾
Whole-territory or whole-system patterns. Governance, cultural coherence, landscape integrity.
Examples: the village's identity over generations, the watershed's ecological regime, regional fishing law.
N4+Bioregional / civilizational▾
The largest scales — biome dynamics, civilizational patterns, planetary cycles.
Examples: Atlantic Forest dynamics, global climate, a civilizational transition.

The key insight: emergence

Within a level, parts add up cleanly: 5 fish breathing = "5 fish breathing." But across levels, parts do NOT add up. A reef ecosystem is not just "many corals doing coral things." New properties emerge — like reef resilience, or self-organizing patches — that don't exist at the level below.

Practical rule: When zooming out one level produces a property that genuinely wasn't there before, you've crossed a LEM boundary. That's where emergence lives — and where most policy interventions miss the mark.

Step 7A Worked Example

Let's apply the whole framework to a real situation.

"We have a coastal fishing village of 300 people. The reef has been declining for 15 years. Young people are leaving. The elders still perform traditional fishing ceremonies."

Processes identified

T1
Daily fishing activity — uniform, ongoing. Any day's fishing is "fishing."
T2
Fish biomass — substance distributed across the reef.
T4
Reef degradation over 15 years — phased, directional. Has a beginning, middle, and a possible "point of no return."
T4
Out-migration of youth — also phased and directional. Each year erodes the next year's possibility space.
T4
Cultural transmission via ceremony — fragile, phased: needs participants at each generation.
T5
The village itself — persistent, organized, spatially bounded. A "thing" composed of all the above.

What the framework reveals

Three T4 (Development) processes are running in parallel and coupled: reef decline, youth migration, ceremony fade. None of them is uniform — each is going somewhere. If you mistake them for T1 activities ("we still fish, we still have ceremonies") you miss the trajectory.

At the LEM level: the N3 process — the village as a self-maintaining socio-ecological system — is losing the conditions for its own continuation. Its parts (N1 fishing, N2 community life) still function, but the whole is no longer recursively self-maintaining.

Leverage insight: Trying to "preserve the ceremony" alone won't work, because the ceremony depends on participants who are leaving because the reef is declining. The intervention has to address the coupling between the T4 processes — not any single one.

Quick reference glossary

Automerity
The degree to which a process is "the same as itself" across smaller portions. A river flowing has high temporal automerity (any second of it is also a flowing-river). Baking a cake has low automerity (the first minute is not "baking a cake").
Homomerity
Whether the parts of a process resemble the whole. Related to automerity but emphasizes structural similarity rather than temporal.
T1 — Activity
A process that is uniform and ongoing — any time-slice IS the same process. Examples: breathing, raining, flowing, fishing (as a daily routine).
T2 — Stuff
A mass or substance, distributed in space without fixed individual location. Examples: water, biomass, sand.
T3 — Amount
A bounded, individuated portion of stuff. Examples: this 50 liters of water, that pile of sand, the harvest of one boat.
T4 — Development
A phased, directional process. Has beginning, middle, end. Cannot be understood from a single snapshot. Examples: a forest succession, a marriage, a degradation, a recovery.
T5 — Thing
A persistent, spatially bounded, highly organized process that we ordinarily call an "object." Trees, villages, organisms, institutions.
LEM levels (N1, N2, N3…)
Levels of organization. Within a level, part-whole relations are transitive (parts add up). Across levels, they are NOT — that's where emergence lives.
Recursively self-maintaining
A process that not only maintains itself, but also maintains the very capacity for self-maintenance. Most healthy ecosystems are recursively self-maintaining. When this property breaks, the system can collapse even if its parts still seem to function.
Emergence
When a higher LEM level has properties that genuinely do not exist at the level below, even though the higher level is composed of the lower level's processes.
Where to go next: Try mapping a real situation you care about. Pick a territory, a community, an organization, or a personal system. Identify 5–8 processes. Classify them. Stack them into LEM levels. Look for the coupled T4 developments — that's usually where the insight is.

Honest flags for classification ambiguity, data gaps, and alternative interpretations — so the output can be trusted.

Real Example — Piracanga Ecovillage, Bahia
Practical Example

Piracanga Ecovillage, Southern Bahia

Groundwater stewardship system · 150 residents · 7-year intentional transformation

Input given to the skill

"An ecovillage in southern Bahia, Brazil — Piracanga — with 150 residents, which has been implementing various processes over the past 7 years with the goal of caring for Water, especially groundwater, which serves as the drinking water supply for all homes. Actions taken include: replacement of sanitation systems (old septic tanks → modern biodigestors), annual water quality analyses, substitution of chemical hygiene and cleaning products with biodegradable alternatives, and active environmental education."

Processes identified & classified

T1 — Activity N1 Groundwater Flow
AutomerityTemporally maximal — any slice IS the same process; spatially maximal
ParticipantsGroundwater (patient/medium); geological substrate (setting); precipitation (agent); recharge zones (instrument)
Dynamic shapeContinuous, sub-cyclic — modulated by rainy/dry seasons, never stopping
ContextEmbedded in regional Atlantic Forest hydrology; sustains the N2 aquifer system
The foundational process the entire intervention is designed to protect. Its health depends on the participant structure remaining uncontaminated.
T2/T3 — Stuff/Amount N1–N2 Groundwater Reserve
AutomeritySpatially maximal as T2 (distributed substance); bounded as T3 when individuated as "the aquifer volume available to the community"
Dynamic shapeStock-and-flow: replenished by recharge (T1), drawn down by extraction (T1), quality modulated by contamination and filtration
ContextEmbedded in the aquifer ecosystem; directly coupled to all contamination-reduction T4 processes
Piracanga's interventions address T2 quality, not T3 quantity — which is the right priority given the described threats.
T4 — Development N2 Sanitation System Transition (fossas → biodigestors)
AutomerityTemporally minimal — diagnosis phase ≠ installation ≠ post-installation monitoring. No slice IS the whole process.
ParticipantsCommunity leadership (agent); old septic systems (patient); biodigestors (new infrastructure); groundwater and soil (beneficiary); technical expertise (instrument)
CompositionContamination diagnosis → technology selection → phased installation → decommissioning of old systems → quality monitoring
Dynamic shapeSequential with irreversible milestones; directional and non-reversible once completed
The structurally most impactful intervention. Old septic tanks were point-source contamination inputs directly into the soil above the aquifer. Biodigestors close that pathway.
T4 — Development N2 Groundwater Quality Recovery
AutomerityTemporally minimal — early recovery ≠ baseline stabilization ≠ restored microbial balance. Cannot be read from a single snapshot.
Dynamic shapeNon-linear, likely asymptotic — rapid early improvement as contamination inputs cease, slower approach to full restoration; threshold effects possible
ContextEnabled by sanitation transition and product substitution (N2); embedded in Atlantic Forest watershed dynamics (N3)
This process is not yet complete. 7 years of monitoring shows trajectory — not arrival. The endpoint (stable safe water sustained without active intervention) has not been confirmed.
T1 + T4 N2 Annual Water Quality Monitoring Program
AutomerityEach annual event is T1 (cyclic snapshot); the 7-year series as a whole is T4 — a developing longitudinal knowledge record
ParticipantsCommunity governance (commissioner); laboratory technicians (executor); water sample as T3 (object); historical baseline (instrument of interpretation)
Dynamic shapeCyclic at event level; linear-accumulative at series level — building a longitudinal dataset that enables trend detection
Does double work: ongoing practice (T1) and the instrument that makes quality recovery (T4) legible. Risk: if results are read as pass/fail instead of as trajectory data, the monitoring underperforms its analytical potential.
T4 — Development N2 Chemical Product Substitution
AutomerityTemporally minimal — awareness ≠ decision ≠ substitution ≠ full adoption and normalization
Dynamic shapeS-curve adoption — rapid among highly motivated residents, slower long tail (visitors, maintenance chemicals, shared facilities)
ContextEnabled by environmental education (N2); embedded in intentional community culture (N3)
Unlike the sanitation transition (clear completion milestones), this T4 has a porous endpoint. Risks stalling at 80–90% substitution — with the remaining chemical load still detectable in sensitive groundwater monitoring.
T4 — Development N2–N3 Environmental Education
AutomerityTemporally minimal — awareness building ≠ knowledge consolidation ≠ behavioral integration ≠ cultural transmission to new members
ParticipantsKnowledgeable residents/educators (agent); all residents, especially newcomers and children (learner); local ecosystem (setting and content)
Dynamic shapeContinuous but uneven — intensive during onboarding and monitoring communications; at risk of dilution with community turnover
The only process that could convert water stewardship from a fragile T4 (developing) into a stable embedded culture (T5). Also the most vulnerable to community membership turnover.
T5 — Thing N3 Piracanga as Socio-Ecological System
AutomeritySpatially bounded (ecovillage territory); temporally persistent but not maximally automerous — the community has changed over 7 years
Participants150 residents (constitutive agents); shared governance (instrument); land and water (setting); shared intention (cohesive force)
ContextEmbedded in regional Atlantic Forest biome (N4); within Brazilian socio-ecological context; within global ecovillage network
Piracanga is functionally self-maintaining in its water stewardship. Whether it has reached recursively self-maintaining status — where the capacity for stewardship is itself transmitted — is the key open question.

Systemic Tensions

⚠ 1
Monitoring logic mismatch (T1 tool reading a T4 process)

Annual analyses produce snapshots (T1 cyclic observation) of a directional recovery process (T4). If results are read as compliance checks ("is it safe this year?") rather than trajectory data ("where are we in the recovery curve?"), the monitoring will fail to detect slow-moving risks — such as a recovery plateau that looks stable but has not reached genuine ecological restoration.

⚠ 2
Environmental education as T4 not yet T5 (institutional fragility)

After 7 years, the community's water stewardship knowledge is still a developing T4 culture, not a stable T5 institution. Community turnover continuously dilutes this capacity unless education becomes a formal, mandatory, documented onboarding process — independent of who carries it.

⚠ 3
Product substitution porous endpoint (incomplete T4)

The substitution process has an S-curve adoption profile with a long tail. High-commitment residents adopted early; visitors, maintenance supplies, and shared spaces continue introducing chemical inputs. A T4 that stalls at 80–90% completion still produces measurable contamination signals.

⚠ 4
Aquifer recovery is slower than community perception

Groundwater systems respond to contamination removal on timescales of years to decades. There is a real risk that the community, seeing improving monitoring results, interprets partial recovery as full recovery and reduces stewardship intensity. Improving ≠ recovered — the system is still within the recovery T4.

Key Leverage Processes

1
Environmental Education (highest leverage)

The only process that operates at N3 and has the potential to make the whole system recursively self-maintaining. If institutionalized, all other interventions become durable. If informal, the system is one leadership change away from gradual erosion.

2
Monitoring as Trajectory Analysis (methodological leverage)

No additional infrastructure needed — only a change in how existing data is used. 7 years of annual analyses constitute a longitudinal dataset. Plotting trends (not just comparing to thresholds) would reveal whether the recovery T4 is on track or plateauing.

3
Procurement & Visitor Protocols (closure leverage)

Closing the long tail of product substitution requires a community policy decision, not individual behavior change. A small institutional effort converts a porous T4 into a permanent reduction in contamination inputs.
